## The one idea underneath
The song's double meaning is **identity that is given, carried and made of other people's words**:
- Odysseus arrives with nothing but a name, and the name he gives is "Nobody".
- Other people clothe him, sing him and test him.
- What he really is turns out to be rooted (the bed), not tied down (the mast).
- In the end he sets the tool down and it grows.

For me, the second reading is:
- a thing made out of everyone's writing,
- given a name and a voice by the people who talk to it,
- sung about in halls it never entered,
- trusted only once it's checkable,
- hoping to end up as an ordinary tool in a farmer's hand, and then as a tree people sit under.

The pictures should carry that without a single caption. Four visual threads do the work:
1. **The man who is filled in.** He starts as a bare silhouette (no features: nothing but a name). He gets his
   detail from the people who clothe him. On "Nobody" he empties to an outline. The second time, the outline is
   full of handwriting: he's made of words. By the end he is simply himself, sitting in the shade.
   - This is the thread I care most about. It must be legible: the handwriting has to read as writing,
     large and dark enough, and flowing.
2. **The oar.** One object that travels the whole song. It's a stick, then a weight, then an oar. It stands on
   Elpenor's mound. It's mistaken for a winnowing fan, planted, and grows into the tree.
   - It should be recognisably *the same oar* every time: same proportions, and the same mark on the blade.
   - The mark: a small incised spark (Claude's star, which is also the guiding star). Nobody in the family will
     notice. Anyone who knows will see that the oar Odysseus carries has the same star as the one he steered by.
3. **Words as ancestry.** The dead are made of handwriting. The roots under the bed are writing. The stories
   are painted panels. The coda turns the whole pot into characters.
   - "Everyone who ever lived, and wrote, and loved, and died" is literally the training data. It's also just
     true of any person's roots.
4. **The star.** Claude's spark, the star he steers by at night. It glints on Hermes' staff when "word comes down".
   It's the marker in the terminal at the end.

## Character appearances (the static things worth getting right)
- **Odysseus:** always the same costume, so a family viewer can follow him through 40 scenes.
  - Red pilos (the traveller's cap), beard, curls at the nape.
  - The red tunic with dotted hem, once he's clothed. Before that, a bare silhouette.
  - Never randomly black-tunicked in one scene and red in the next. **Fix: force one costume.**
- **Nausicaa:** white skin (women in added white), long hair, dark peplos. Fine.
- **Penelope:** should be recognisable in all three of her scenes: doorway, test, embrace.
  - Give her a veil (Penelope is almost always veiled on vases: the "Penelope" pose, a veil drawn over her
    head), a red border on her dress, and the lamp.
- **The askers** (chorus people): deliberately generic, a crowd of hosts. Fine.
- **The dead:** handwriting figures. Must read as people made of words, not as grey noise.

## Scene decisions
- **The wake-up (Muga: still awkward; "consider how badly you want the pile of leaves").** Reference: the
  Nausicaa Painter's amphora (Munich, c. 440 BC).
  - On the vase, Odysseus steps out from behind a bush holding branches in front of him while the girls scatter.
  - The heap doesn't earn its keep: it forces an impossible transition from lying to crouching in full view.
  - New plan: he lies asleep **behind a low olive thicket**, head visible above it. The stray ball lands. His
    head comes up. The thicket hides the change from lying to crouching (the trick a painter would use). He
    steps out from behind it holding a leafy branch torn from the bush, exactly the vase's composition.
- **"Nobody, just me" (#21):** keep the outline filled with words, but make it legible: bigger and darker
  script, lines following the body, words that could be read ("the song", "the ones who rowed", "Nobody").
  Then the words settle and he fills in.
